F7 What drives error most — country, crop, or model choice?

The country matters most. Which country alone explains ~82% of variation in log NRMSE; which crop adds ~8%; model choice adds only ~3%. Together, "which country and which crop" accounts for 90.3%. This holds even when tabular models are included — the task dominance is a property of the data, not the model family.

MaizeCountry dominates maize variance even more sharply. African maize (AO, BF, ET, ML, TD) drives the high-NRMSE tail — median NRMSE 0.44, skill vs rolling-3 −23.5%. European maize clusters tightly at ~0.20 NRMSE. Country alone predicts whether the task will be easy or hard with ~85% reliability for maize.

WheatCountry explains less variance for wheat because European wheat tasks are more homogeneous (most NRMSE 0.09–0.25). High-error outliers: AU wheat (0.52) and some Asian markets (CN 0.28). Country is a weaker but still dominant predictor for wheat compared to maize.

🌽 Maize insight Country still dominates for maize (~82%). Africa's raw NRMSE is ~0.44 (maize), 2× Europe's 0.21. US maize NRMSE is 0.19 but rolling-3 skill is −8 to −12% due to very strong yield persistence.
🌾 Wheat insight Country still dominates for wheat. European wheat has lowest NRMSE (~0.20). Australia wheat shows high NRMSE (~0.47) driven by rainfall variability and n=1 country in Oceania.

The practical implication: improving from the worst architecture (dlinear) to the best combo (tabpfn·dekad) moves the needle by approximately 4–5 percentage points of explained variance. Improving dataset coverage — more countries, longer time series, better features — could unlock the other 90%. This benchmark is as much about understanding data difficulty as it is about comparing models.

F10

F10 Which architecture and resolution should you use for your research experiments?

Use patchtst at dekad (10-day) resolution as your primary trained model. Pair it with tabpfn at dekad resolution as your zero-training baseline — it is the strongest single combo in the benchmark and sets a meaningful bar that your research enhancements need to beat.

Primary: patchtst · dekad

For your research architecture — adding features (RTAI, soil, lag years), exploring pooling, running ablations:

  • #1 Friedman rank among all trained sequence models at non-daily resolution (8.59 of 17 combos)
  • Skill −7.2% vs rolling-3 — nearly 5 points better than patchtst·daily (−12.3%)
  • Free efficiency win: 36 timesteps vs 365 (1/10th the sequence length), so sweeps run 10× faster
  • #1 in wheat, tied-#1 in maize — most crop-balanced of the trained models
  • Scales best with data — transformers benefit most from additional samples and features

Zero-training baseline: tabpfn · dekad

Always run this alongside your trained model as a sanity check:

  • Best combo in the entire benchmark (Friedman r̄ 6.56 of 17 combos)
  • Skill −4.5% vs rolling-3 — better than patchtst·dekad (−7.2%), with no training
  • Beats climatology (+2.0% skill) — the first combo to clear this bar
  • Beats patchtst·daily in 58% of tasks head-to-head
  • In Europe: skill vs rolling-3 = −0.4% — essentially matching the rolling mean
  • Use it to frame the question: does your domain knowledge (RTAI, soil, lag years) add more than what tabpfn gets from seasonal summaries alone?
What to watch for in your experiments:
  • Report skill vs rolling-3 as your primary metric. NRMSE alone can mislead (tabicl·monthly has NRMSE 0.227 but skill_roll3 −10.1%). A model that lowers NRMSE but worsens skill_roll3 is capturing less than the baseline.
  • Test whether your domain features (RTAI, RUE, soil) help patchtst more than tabpfn. tabpfn operates on season-level summaries — if your features add sub-seasonal signal, only patchtst can exploit it.
  • If you later run multi-task or pooled experiments, re-check the per-resolution ranking — pooling changes the effective sample size and may alter whether daily vs dekad is optimal.
  • tabicl has the lowest absolute NRMSE (0.227 at monthly) — worth checking if your research prioritizes absolute accuracy over baseline-relative skill.
  • mitra was excluded from all comparisons (NRMSE > 1.5); tabdpt underperforms consistently (see F6).

F11 Do country clusters explain model performance patterns?

Yes. Grouping by Koppen climate zone reveals distinct model preference patterns. Temperate (C) is the best zone for all models: tabpfn·dekad reaches −0.1% skill vs rolling-3 (essentially matching rolling-3), and patchtst reaches −4.5%. Tropical (A): LPJmL rainfed achieves +6.2% skill (the only positive value), while tabpfn and trained models all struggle (−16 to −28%). Continental/Boreal (D): LPJmL and tabpfn are both around −16% — persistent CEE yield trends and cold-season physiology limit all data-driven models. Arid/Semi-arid (B): tabpfn struggles most (−31.5%) with only 4 tasks (ZA, KZ, TR, AU).

LPJmL rainfed has a unique process-knowledge advantage in Tropical zones (+6.2% skill) where it leverages plant physiology that data-driven models cannot infer from 10–20 years of data. In Arid/Semi-arid zones LPJmL severely underperforms (−58.7%) due to irrigation assumptions mismatch. tabpfn·dekad nearly matches the rolling-3 baseline in Temperate zones (−0.1%) — this is the zone where the benchmark is "solved." For Continental/Boreal, all models fail similarly, suggesting the fundamental challenge is yield level trend (not feature quality).

Connection to F7 (task dominance): The finding that 90% of variance is explained by which country/crop (F7) is actually driven by within-zone differences — high-shifting CEE countries (LT, CZ, BG) drag down the Continental median, while stable Western European countries pull up the Temperate median. Zone membership and intra-zone heterogeneity compound to produce the extreme task-level spread observed in F7.

F12 Where does LPJmL add value over ML models?

LPJmL rainfed beats patchtst in 23 of 51 country×crop pairs where both are available. It is especially competitive in Continental/Boreal wheat (BE +27pp, DE +14pp, FI +8pp over patchtst) and Central European maize (HU +27pp, CZ +16pp, DE +14pp). It underperforms in Tropical countries and in countries with high yield distribution shift (DK maize KS=0.80, LT maize KS=0.62), where the bias correction cannot fully compensate for rapid yield level changes.

F2

At daily resolution, can any model beat the 3-year rolling average for maize?

No — not even close. Maize patchtst·daily skill vs rolling-3 = −16.3%. Models beat the Mann-Kendall trend baseline (+1.8%) but lose to OLS trend (−5.2%) and are essentially tied with the historical climatology mean (−0.7%). The rolling-3 wins in 71% of maize tasks.

Why maize is so much harder than wheat: Rapid yield trends driven by variety adoption (LT +99%, ET +71%, BG +57%, DK +40%) mean the target variable jumps sharply upward in the test period. The rolling-3 uses the 3 most recent training years — already partially adapted to this trend — while trained models fit the full historical distribution and lag behind. Models lose to rolling-3 in 71% of maize tasks vs 67% of wheat tasks.
F3

Does changing input resolution help for maize?

Yes — dekad aggregation is the best resolution for patchtst (−12.6% vs −16.3% at daily), a meaningful 3.7pp improvement. xlinear benefits most from monthly aggregation (−9.7%). The noise-filtering benefit of coarser time steps is especially valuable for maize where daily weather noise is high relative to the growing-season signal.

patchtst·dekad is the best trained-model–resolution combo for maize (−12.6% skill). It runs at 1/10th the wall time of daily (36 vs 365 timesteps) and achieves better skill. For maize sweeps, always prefer dekad over daily for patchtst.
xlinear monthly (−9.7%) is the best linear model for maize. Monthly aggregation extracts more signal from seasonal summaries than sub-monthly dynamics, and xlinear's low-variance fitting handles the reduced sequence well. For quick baselines on maize, xlinear·monthly is an efficient choice.

Does more training data reliably improve maize forecasting skill?

Non-monotone. The best maize quartile is Q2 (42–80 samples; CZ, PL, LT, BE maize) with skill −1.3% — mid-sized European countries where models actually learn beyond the recent average. The worst are Q1 (small EU outliers, −17.3%) and Q4 (US/BR/AR data-rich but persistence-dominated, −16.5%). More data raises the bar as well as the model.

Q4 maize (US, BR, AR, DE, FR) is not the easiest despite having the most data — these are also countries with strong yield persistence (US corn belt, Brazilian soy rotation) where even many training samples cannot break the rolling-3's momentum advantage. Data quantity helps only when task difficulty does not scale with it.

Which continents are hardest for wheat, and does tabpfn help?

Asia (CN, IN wheat) is by far the hardest: patchtst·daily skill = −35.2% vs rolling-3 — the worst of any continent for any crop. China's wheat yields are extremely persistent (driven by state procurement prices, not weather), making the rolling-3 nearly unbeatable. In contrast, North American wheat (US) is the easiest: patchtst·daily actually beats the rolling-3 (+0.5%).

Does more training data reliably improve wheat skill?

Yes — but only once you cross the Q3 threshold (~80 samples). Q3 (AU, CN, EL, ES, HU, RO: 78–205 samples) and Q4 (AR, BR, DE, FR, IN, IT, US: 412–4393 samples) both achieve excellent skill (−1.2% and −1.8%). Q1 and Q2 — small Baltic states, Baltic/CEE wheat — are poor (−31% and −21%), mainly because these are the countries with the highest yield distribution shift (CZ, LT, BG, HU) where test-period yields jumped far above training levels.

Q2 wheat is worse than Q1 for wheat (−21% vs −31%). Q2 includes CZ, DK, FI, PL, SE wheat — small-to-medium European countries with moderate data, BUT these are also the countries with the highest yield distribution shift (CZ wheat KS=0.64, DK wheat KS≈0.47). The rolling-3 adapts to their fast-rising yield trends while trained models fit the historical distribution. Data quantity doesn't help when the target variable drifts systematically.

Notes & methods

  • Architecture sweep: 14 models × 62 tasks (35 maize + 27 wheat countries, ≥10 years) at daily resolution. 868 runs total.
  • Resolution sweep: patchtst and xlinear at all 4 resolutions; tabpfn, tabicl, tabdpt at weekly/dekad/monthly only. 62 tasks per combo.
  • Friedman rank (crop-specific): ranked per country on skill_rmse_vs_rolling3 (1=best), then averaged — computed separately for maize (35 countries) and wheat (27 countries).
  • Skill formula: skill = 1 − RMSE_model / RMSE_reference. Positive = model beats the reference. Rolling-3 = 3-year rolling mean of observed yields (most recent 3 training years per region).
  • Winner per task: among {tabpfn·dekad, patchtst·dekad, xlinear·daily, patchtst·daily, LPJmL rainfed} the combo with highest skill_rmse_vs_rolling3 wins that country×crop task.
  • Data: CY-Bench v1.0, end-of-season horizon, 5-year fixed test split. mitra excluded (NRMSE > 1.5).
