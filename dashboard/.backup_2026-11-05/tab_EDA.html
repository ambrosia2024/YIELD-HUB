What the variable shift data tells you

Five findings from the distribution shift analysis — comparing test-period (last 5 years) vs training-period distributions across 13 variables for every crop–country pair. KS statistic measures whether distributions changed in shape; Wasserstein measures how far the mass moved in original units.

  • E1Yield is the most shifted variable — test-period yields are systematically higher in 86% of countries, reflecting a combination of technology trend and climate response.
  • E2Temperature is warming across the board: tmax +0.66°C, tavg +0.56°C, tmin +0.43°C median. The warming signal is real, consistent, and not confined to one region.
  • E3Countries with high yield shift have worse skill vs rolling-3 (r=−0.24). The rolling-3 baseline adapts to yield level shifts; trained models do not.
  • E4Central and Eastern Europe has the steepest yield distribution shifts — HU, CZ, RO, LT, BG top the list. These are also some of the benchmark's hardest countries for model skill.
  • E5Precipitation barely changes (+0.3%) but climate water balance (CWB) is declining (−11%). The drying signal comes from ET₀ increasing, not from less rain.
E1

Yield is the most shifted variable across all crops and countries

Yield has the highest median KS statistic of all 13 variables: 0.38 for maize, 0.32 for wheat. Test-period yields are higher than training-period yields in 86% of country–crop pairs. This is a strong, consistent upward trend in the target variable itself.

Implication for model training: When the target variable (yield) is systematically higher in the test period than training, any model that fits the training distribution will tend to under-predict. The rolling-3 baseline is immune to this because it uses only the most recent training years — it has already adapted to the upward trend.

Most extreme yield shift: DK maize KS=0.80 (mean train 5.1 t/ha → mean test 7.2 t/ha), HU wheat KS=0.71 (4.0 → 5.4 t/ha), ET maize KS=0.69 (1.7 → 3.0 t/ha — driven by adoption of improved varieties).

E2

Temperature is warming consistently: +0.66°C tmax, +0.56°C tavg, +0.43°C tmin

The mean shift in temperature variables is not noise — it is a systematic warming across all crops and continents. tmax has the largest absolute shift (+0.66°C median); tmin the smallest (+0.43°C), consistent with greenhouse gas amplification patterns. VPD also increases (+4.8%), reflecting higher evaporative demand.

Heat-stress responses and drought signals that were rare in training become more common in test years. This is an inherent OOD challenge for models relying on training-time weather statistics.
E3

Yield distribution shift predicts worse rolling-3 skill (r = −0.24)

Countries with higher yield KS statistic (more shifted test-period yields) show significantly worse rolling-3 skill: correlation r = −0.24 (p < 0.05). This is not a coincidence — the rolling-3 baseline uses the most recent 3 training years, which are closer to the test period and partially reflect the yield trend. Trained models fit the full training distribution and are less adaptive.

This mechanistically explains why the rolling-3 is unbeaten. It is not that the rolling-3 is a magical forecaster — it is that yield trends mean the last 3 years are systematically a better predictor than the full training distribution mean. Any model that cannot adapt its prediction level to recent yield trends will lose to the rolling-3 in countries with strong yield growth.
Implication for the benchmark: The hardest countries in benchmarking (DK, ET, HU, LT for maize; HU, CZ, RO for wheat) are almost exactly the countries with the highest yield KS statistic. Architecture choice matters less here than the model's ability to track yield level trends — which all current architectures lack without lag features.
E4

Central and Eastern Europe has the steepest yield shifts

The top yield-shifted countries are predominantly Central/Eastern European: HU (wheat KS=0.71), CZ (0.64), RO (0.61 wheat, 0.57 maize), LT (0.62 maize, 0.45 wheat), BG (0.64 maize, 0.49 wheat). These countries saw rapid yield improvement post-2000 from agricultural intensification and improved varieties — not primarily from climate.

E5

CWB is declining despite stable precipitation — ET₀ is the driver

Climate water balance (CWB = precipitation − ET₀) shows a median decline of −11% in test vs training period. Precipitation itself barely changes (+0.3%). The drying signal comes from increasing ET₀ (+2.2%), which in turn comes from warmer temperatures and higher VPD. This is a subtle but important distinction: drought stress is increasing even where rainfall is stable.

For crop yield forecasting: CWB is a more informative stress indicator than raw precipitation alone. The declining CWB in the test period means test conditions are drier than training in a water-demand sense, even where rainfall statistics look identical. Models using only precipitation as a water feature may miss this systematic shift.
Overlooked implication: The variables that show high test-period shift (yield, tmax, fpar, et0, vpd) are linked — warming → higher et0 and vpd → higher water stress → potential yield impact. FPAR (vegetation photosynthetic capacity) also increasing in test period suggests vegetation response to both CO₂ fertilisation and earlier seasons. These co-occurring signals make it difficult for a model to attribute yield gains to any single channel.
