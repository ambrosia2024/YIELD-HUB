F2

F2 At daily resolution, does any model beat the 3-year rolling average?

No — not on median. Every architecture has negative median skill vs rolling-3 at daily resolution. patchtst, the best, beats it in only 31% of tasks. Models beat the Mann-Kendall (Theil-Sen) trend baseline (+4.4% skill for patchtst) but lose to the OLS linear trend (−4.1%). The OLS line captures smooth, long-run yield growth that models only partially replicate from weather inputs; the Theil-Sen slope is noisier and easier to beat. The rolling mean remains the hardest bar.

The rolling-3 undefeated streak at daily resolution: A 3-year rolling average needs no model, no weather data, and no training. The fact that every architecture fails this test means the forecast improvements from deep learning on daily weather are smaller than the year-to-year momentum signal already in the yield data. This is not a deficiency of the models — it reflects the data generating process. Resolution change (F3) and tabular models (F4–F5) partially, but not fully, resolve this.
EDA confirms why rolling-3 is unbeatable: Variable shift analysis shows test-period yields are systematically higher than training in 86% of countries (median yield KS = 0.38 for maize, 0.32 for wheat). Countries with greater yield distribution shift have significantly worse rolling-3 skill (r = −0.24). The rolling-3 uses the 3 most recent training years — already partially adapted to the yield upward trend — while trained models fit the full historical distribution and lag behind. See the EDA tab for per-country breakdowns.

The hierarchy: rolling-3 (hardest) ≫ climatology ≈ best model > OLS trend > best model > Mann-Kendall/Theil-Sen trend (easiest). Models sit between the two trend baselines — beating the nonparametric Theil-Sen slope but losing to the fitted OLS line. Models sit between the two trend baselines in absolute terms, but struggle against the rolling-3's momentum signal. Resolution and tabular models (F3–F5) shift this hierarchy meaningfully.

Resolution — where sequence models work best

F3

F3 Does input resolution change the picture for the best sequence models?

Yes — but selectively. patchtst·dekad nearly halves its skill deficit vs rolling-3 (from −12.3% to −7.2%). xlinear barely changes: its best is monthly at −8.7%, only 1 point better than daily. The improvement from switching resolution is architecture-dependent.

patchtst·dekad is the best trained-model–resolution combo. Skill −7.2% vs rolling-3 (compared to −12.3% daily) — the dekad aggregation seems to filter weather noise while preserving the dominant growing-season signal that patchtst attends to with its patch-based tokenisation. The 10-day window likely aligns with meaningful phenological cycles.

The crop breakdown reveals an important asymmetry: wheat patchtst improves sharply at dekad (−3.2% daily → −4.9% dekad, nearly flat — wheat skill is already decent). For maize, the gains are smaller but still consistent at dekad. xlinear's best resolution for both crops is monthly, suggesting xlinear extracts more signal from seasonal-level summaries than from sub-monthly dynamics.

Efficiency implication: patchtst at dekad (36 timesteps) runs at roughly 1/10th the wall time of daily (365 steps), and achieves better skill. For large research sweeps across many countries and feature ablations, this is a free performance upgrade. Strongly prefer dekad over daily for patchtst experiments.

Where models work

F7

F7 What drives error most — country, crop, or model choice?

The country matters most. Which country alone explains ~82% of variation in log NRMSE; which crop adds ~8%; model choice adds only ~3%. Together, "which country and which crop" accounts for 90.3%. This holds even when tabular models are included — the task dominance is a property of the data, not the model family.

The practical implication: improving from the worst architecture (dlinear) to the best combo (tabpfn·dekad) moves the needle by approximately 4–5 percentage points of explained variance. Improving dataset coverage — more countries, longer time series, better features — could unlock the other 90%. This benchmark is as much about understanding data difficulty as it is about comparing models.

Asia (India, China) looks moderate in absolute error but has the worst skill vs rolling-3 — yields there are so persistent that the rolling mean is nearly unbeatable, even for tabpfn. tabpfn helps most in Europe, where it nearly eliminates the rolling-3 gap.

Data effects

F9

F9 Does more training data reliably improve model skill?

Not in a simple way. Raw NRMSE does not correlate with sample count (r = 0.09). Skill vs rolling-3 improves weakly with more data (r = 0.16), but the effect is non-monotone: the third data quartile (not the fourth) has the best skill. Data-rich tasks include some of the hardest countries.

How quartiles are defined: Sample quartiles (Q1–Q4) are defined by sorting all 62 country-crop tasks by total training samples. Q1 = fewest samples (data-scarce African countries and small island nations). Q2 = second quartile. Q3 = third quartile. Q4 = most samples (US, India, China with many administrative regions, all with long time series). The figure shows how skill vs rolling-3 varies across these groups.

Q4 (data-richest: US, India, China) has worse skill than Q3 because these countries also have the strongest rolling-3 baselines (yield persistence). More data raises the bar as well as the model's capability. The clean implication: data quantity alone is not a reliable predictor of model performance; data-relative-to-baseline-difficulty is what matters.

Transformer advantage on data-rich tasks: When tasks are split by sample size, transformers (especially patchtst) show a steeper improvement curve than linear models as data increases. For research experiments on data-rich countries (US, EU), patchtst is the clearer choice over xlinear. For data-scarce African countries, the gap narrows and xlinear may suffice.

Architecture verdict

F10

F10 Which architecture and resolution should you use for your research experiments?

Use patchtst at dekad (10-day) resolution as your primary trained model. Pair it with tabpfn at dekad resolution as your zero-training baseline — it is the strongest single combo in the benchmark and sets a meaningful bar that your research enhancements need to beat.

Primary: patchtst · dekad

For your research architecture — adding features (RTAI, soil, lag years), exploring pooling, running ablations:

  • #1 Friedman rank among all trained sequence models at non-daily resolution (8.59 of 17 combos)
  • Skill −7.2% vs rolling-3 — nearly 5 points better than patchtst·daily (−12.3%)
  • Free efficiency win: 36 timesteps vs 365 (1/10th the sequence length), so sweeps run 10× faster
  • #1 in wheat, tied-#1 in maize — most crop-balanced of the trained models
  • Scales best with data — transformers benefit most from additional samples and features

Zero-training baseline: tabpfn · dekad

Always run this alongside your trained model as a sanity check:

  • Best combo in the entire benchmark (Friedman r̄ 6.56 of 17 combos)
  • Skill −4.5% vs rolling-3 — better than patchtst·dekad (−7.2%), with no training
  • Beats climatology (+2.0% skill) — the first combo to clear this bar
  • Beats patchtst·daily in 58% of tasks head-to-head
  • In Europe: skill vs rolling-3 = −0.4% — essentially matching the rolling mean
  • Use it to frame the question: does your domain knowledge (RTAI, soil, lag years) add more than what tabpfn gets from seasonal summaries alone?
What to watch for in your experiments:
  • Report skill vs rolling-3 as your primary metric. NRMSE alone can mislead (tabicl·monthly has NRMSE 0.227 but skill_roll3 −10.1%). A model that lowers NRMSE but worsens skill_roll3 is capturing less than the baseline.
  • Test whether your domain features (RTAI, RUE, soil) help patchtst more than tabpfn. tabpfn operates on season-level summaries — if your features add sub-seasonal signal, only patchtst can exploit it.
  • If you later run multi-task or pooled experiments, re-check the per-resolution ranking — pooling changes the effective sample size and may alter whether daily vs dekad is optimal.
  • tabicl has the lowest absolute NRMSE (0.227 at monthly) — worth checking if your research prioritizes absolute accuracy over baseline-relative skill.
  • mitra was excluded from all comparisons (NRMSE > 1.5); tabdpt underperforms consistently (see F6).

Yes. Grouping by Koppen climate zone reveals distinct model preference patterns. Continental/Boreal countries (D) are where LPJmL rainfed outperforms patchtst (+8.7pp); Tropical countries (A) are where xlinear outperforms patchtst (−6.1% vs −21.1%); Temperate (C) is the best overall zone for all models (−6.9% patchtst). Model choice matters more in some zones than others.

LPJmL rainfed has a process-knowledge advantage in Continental/Boreal zones (Central/Eastern Europe, Baltic states) where crop physiology — vernalization, cold-limited seasons — matters. xlinear handles Tropical best: likely because Tropical countries have sparse data where a linear model's lower variance beats complex architectures.

Connection to F7 (task dominance): The finding that 90% of variance is explained by which country/crop (F7) is actually driven by within-zone differences — high-shifting CEE countries (LT, CZ, BG) drag down the Continental median, while stable Western European countries pull up the Temperate median. Zone membership and intra-zone heterogeneity compound to produce the extreme task-level spread observed in F7.
F12

F12 Where does LPJmL add value over ML models?

LPJmL rainfed beats patchtst in 23 of 51 country×crop pairs where both are available. It is especially competitive in Continental/Boreal wheat (BE +27pp, DE +14pp, FI +8pp over patchtst) and Central European maize (HU +27pp, CZ +16pp, DE +14pp). It underperforms in Tropical countries and in countries with high yield distribution shift (DK maize KS=0.80, LT maize KS=0.62), where the bias correction cannot fully compensate for rapid yield level changes.

Notes & methods

  • Architecture sweep: 14 models × 62 tasks (35 maize + 27 wheat countries, ≥10 years) at daily resolution, B0 config (CWB features only, MQL loss, lag_years=0). 868 runs total.
  • Resolution sweep: patchtst and xlinear at all 4 resolutions (daily/weekly/dekad/monthly); tabpfn, tabicl, tabdpt, mitra at weekly/dekad/monthly only (no daily — tabFM loads at daily internally and bins down). 62 tasks per combo. mitra excluded from all comparisons (NRMSE > 1.5, catastrophically broken).
  • Friedman rank (17 combos): computed by ranking all 17 model×resolution combos per country on skill_rmse_vs_rolling3 (1=best), then averaging across countries. tabpfn takes slots #1–3 (monthly/dekad/weekly); best trained-model combo is xlinear·daily at #6.
  • Skill formula: skill = 1 − RMSE_model / RMSE_reference. Positive = model beats the reference. Rolling-3 = 3-year rolling mean of observed yields (most recent 3 training years per region). Climatology = historical mean per region across all training years.
  • Variance decomposition: one-way SS ratio for log-transformed NRMSE. Country×crop combined as a single task factor.
  • Head-to-head count (tabpfn·dekad vs patchtst·daily): per-task comparison across 62 tasks; tabpfn·dekad wins on skill_rmse_vs_rolling3 in 36 of 62 tasks (58%).
  • tabpfn·monthly Friedman rank paradox: best Friedman rank (#1, 6.49) but worse median skill_roll3 (−10.1%) than tabpfn·dekad (−4.5%). Monthly tabpfn is consistently competitive (ranks high per country) but has more variance in its per-task skill, dragging the median below dekad.
  • Data: CY-Bench v1.0, end-of-season horizon, 5-year fixed test split. Evaluation via YIELD-HUB process/metrics.py tier system.
